LLM Judge — CI Gating & Human Disagreement Notes
=================================================

What confidence number would I trust enough to block a build?
----------------------------------------------------------------
The "confidence" score is just the AI telling us how sure IT feels about its
own answer — it's not proof that the answer is actually correct. So I
wouldn't use it as a single magic number like "block if confidence < 0.8."
That gives a false sense of precision.

Instead, think of it as two separate signals working together — did it say
PASS or FAIL, and was it sure or unsure:

  - FAIL + the AI is confident  -> block the build. This is the trustworthy
    case: the AI found a real problem and is sure about it.

  - FAIL + the AI is NOT confident -> don't block automatically. Flag it for
    a person to glance at instead. If even the AI isn't sure, it's probably
    a borderline or confusing case, not a real bug — auto-blocking on these
    just trains the team to stop trusting red builds.

  - PASS -> let the build go through either way. But if it passed while the
    AI seemed unsure, quietly log it so someone can spot-check it later.

It's a bit like a spell-checker: if it's very sure a word is wrong, it flags
it right away; if it's unsure, it just underlines it for you to glance at
later — it doesn't stop you from saving the document. I'd start around
"confident = 0.8 or higher" and adjust that number after watching it in
action for a few weeks.

What if the AI judge and a human reviewer disagree?
-------------------------------------------------------
The human always wins. The AI is a quick first-pass helper, not the final
word. In practice:

1. Write down every disagreement — what the script said, what was asked,
   what the AI decided, and what the human decided. Don't just argue about
   it once and move on.
2. Don't react to just one disagreement. Wait and see if there's a pattern
   — is the AI always too easy-going on the same kind of question? That
   usually means our instructions to the AI were unclear, not that the AI
   is broken.
3. If there's a pattern, fix the instructions we give the AI first. That's
   a more lasting fix than fiddling with a confidence number.
4. If one specific question keeps causing arguments even after we've
   clarified the instructions, it's probably not a good fit for AI
   judgment at all — replace it with a simple, plain rule in code instead
   (something the computer can check directly, no guessing involved).
